ARPANET: the story

    Site map

    Four computers walk into a lab.

    On October 29, 1969, a student at UCLA tried to send one word to a computer 350 miles away. This is the story of what happened next, and why it changed everything from mail to shopping to arguing with strangers.

    The first message sent over ARPANET was “LO”, the start of “LOGIN”, before the system crashed.
    In plain English

    ARPANET was the first network that let computers in different places send messages to each other. It was the great-grandparent of the internet. This site explains how it started, who built it, and why its first message was cut off after two letters.

    Charley Kline was typing “LOGIN” to a colleague at Stanford Research Institute (SRI). The receiving system fell over after two letters. The first words ever sent across a computer network were, by accident, “lo.” As in “lo and behold.” History has a sense of humor and no sense of timing.

    The argument of this site

    ARPANET was not built to survive a nuclear war. It was built to solve a sharing problem: expensive computers sat in separate rooms, and researchers wanted to use them without flying across the country. The solution, packet switching on a cooperative network, became the foundation of the internet.

    Origins: a satellite, an agency, a headache

    In plain English

    In the 1960s every research computer was an island. A scientist who wanted to use a machine in another city had to travel there or mail boxes of data. ARPANET was the bridge between those islands.

    In October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik. In February 1958 the United States created the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) to make sure it was never surprised again. Surprise was now a line item.

    The problem: computers did not talk

    By the mid-1960s ARPA funded computers at universities and labs across the country. Each machine had its own terminals, its own software, and its own language. Bob Taylor, who ran ARPA’s computing office, had three different terminals in his office, each wired to a different computer. He needed all three, and they could not talk to one another. He later described wanting to fix that.

    The idea: an intergalactic network

    J.C.R. Licklider, the first head of that office, circulated a memo in 1963 addressed to “Members and Affiliates of the Intergalactic Computer Network.” It was not a joke. It was a plan, delivered in the tone of a joke, which is the best way to get a plan funded.

    The method: packets

    Two researchers, Paul Baran in the United States and Donald Davies in Britain, separately arrived at the idea of cutting messages into small blocks and routing each one independently. Davies called the blocks “packets.” The name stuck, probably because “message blocks” sounds like a toy.

    The myth: was it built for nuclear war?

    Myth: ARPANET was designed to survive a nuclear attack.

    Better version: Baran’s RAND research did concern survivable communications, and it influenced the field. But the people who built ARPANET, including Larry Roberts and Bob Taylor, described its goal as resource sharing. Historian Janet Abbate discusses this distinction in Inventing the Internet.

    Three dates to remember

    1969: the first message between two computers. 1971: the first email. 1983: the shared rules (TCP/IP) that the modern internet still uses.

    1. 1957

      Sputnik launches. American officials discover they are behind and would like to talk to someone about it.

    2. 1958

      ARPA is created. It will be renamed DARPA in 1972, adding “Defense” and a letter to every business card.

    3. 1960

      Licklider publishes “Man-Computer Symbiosis,” imagining people and computers thinking together.

    4. 1963

      Licklider’s “Intergalactic Computer Network” memo.

    5. 1964

      Baran publishes On Distributed Communications at RAND.

    6. 1966

      Taylor hires Larry Roberts to build the network.

    7. 1967

      Roberts presents his plan in Gatlinburg, Tennessee. A British team at the same meeting describes packet switching, and Roberts plans for much faster lines (50 kilobits per second).

    8. 1968

      ARPA asks for bids. Some large companies doubt it will work. A small Massachusetts firm, Bolt Beranek and Newman (BBN), says it will. BBN wins.

    9. Sep 1969

      The first Interface Message Processor (IMP) arrives at UCLA. It is about the size of a refrigerator and weighs roughly 900 pounds.

    10. Oct 29, 1969

      UCLA to SRI. The message is “LO.” The crash is free of charge.

    11. Dec 1969

      Four nodes are online: UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

    12. 1971

      The network reaches 15 nodes. Ray Tomlinson at BBN sends networked email and picks the @ sign.

    13. 1972

      A public demonstration in Washington, D.C. shows the network to skeptics, who become somewhat fewer.

    14. 1973

      The first international links connect Norway and London.

    15. 1974

      Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn publish the design for TCP, the seed of TCP/IP.

    16. Jan 1, 1983

      ARPANET switches to TCP/IP. It is called “flag day.” Nobody gets the day off.

    17. 1990

      ARPANET is shut down. The network it started is just getting going.

    How it worked

    In plain English

    Imagine mailing a long letter one page at a time. Each page goes in its own numbered envelope and takes whatever road is open. The receiver puts the pages in order. That is packet switching: the pages are packets, the numbered label is a header, and the roads are the network.

    A phone call reserves one whole line from start to finish, even during the long silences. That is fine for talking. It is wasteful for computers, which send short bursts of data and then sit quietly.

    Packet switching reserves nothing. The message is cut into pieces, each piece gets a header, and the network delivers them however it can. Many conversations share the same lines.

    The original four nodes

    Each site had an IMP, a ruggedized minicomputer that handled the network chores so the host computer did not have to. The IMPs talked over leased 50-kilobit phone lines. A typical three-megabyte phone photo would need about eight minutes to cross one.

    Try it: send a message in packets


    Notice that the packets can arrive out of order. The receiving IMP sorts them. That is the entire trick, and it is why a network can lose a line and carry on.

    The rules got written down, politely

    In April 1969 Steve Crocker, then a UCLA graduate student, wrote a note titled “Host Software.” It was Request for Comments number 1. He called them “requests” so nobody felt ordered around. The internet still uses the format.

    People

    In plain English

    Click a name to see what they did. You do not need to memorize everyone. If you remember three: Licklider had the idea, Roberts made the plan, and Kline typed the first message.

    J.C.R. Licklider

    First head of ARPA’s computing office. Imagined the network, then funded the people who could build it.

    Bob Taylor

    Ran the office from 1966, had three terminals he wanted to unify, and hired Roberts.

    Larry Roberts

    Designed the ARPANET plan and managed its construction.

    Leonard Kleinrock

    UCLA professor whose work on queueing theory helped explain how packet networks behave. His lab hosted the first node.

    Paul Baran and Donald Davies

    Independently developed packet switching in the United States and Britain. Davies named the packet.

    Frank Heart and the BBN team

    Built the IMPs on a tight schedule and made them reliable enough to leave unattended.

    Charley Kline and Bill Duvall

    Kline typed at UCLA. Duvall waited at SRI. Between them they produced “LO.”

    Steve Crocker

    Started the RFC tradition and a culture of open, friendly technical argument.

    Elizabeth “Jake” Feinler

    Ran the Network Information Center at SRI, kept the directory of who was on the network, and helped create domain categories such as .com and .edu.

    Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn

    Designed TCP/IP so separate networks could join into one.

    Legacy

    In plain English

    ARPANET began as a small network for researchers. Other networks copied its ideas and joined together, and that big joined-up network is the internet.

    From “LO” to everything

    ARPANET connected research sites, and then it connected the way people worked. Email, first sent in 1971, soon became the main thing on the network. The designers expected remote computing. Their users, being people, mostly wanted to send messages. This remains the most reliable prediction in the history of technology.

    What changed, and what stayed

    By 1981 the network had about 213 host computers. In 1983 it moved to TCP/IP and a separate military network, MILNET, split off. By 1990 ARPANET had been retired, replaced by the larger network it had taught the world to build.

    Why it still matters

    The design choices of ARPANET are still visible today: open standards, small cooperative pieces, and rules written as requests for comments. Its builders wanted to share computers. They produced the habit of sharing nearly everything else.

    Discussion question for a paper or exhibit: A network built for scientists became a public utility. What does that suggest about how technologies find their real purpose?

    Glossary

    Plain definitions of the hard words. No technical background needed.

    network
    Computers connected so they can send data to each other.
    ARPA
    The U.S. government agency that paid for the project. Today it is called DARPA.
    node
    One location on the network. In 1969 each node was a university or lab.
    host
    The main computer at a node that people actually used.
    IMP
    Interface Message Processor. A small computer at each node that sent and received packets so the host did not have to. About the size of a refrigerator.
    packet
    A small piece of a message, sent on its own and put back together at the end.
    header
    The label on a packet: where it came from, where it is going, and its number in the sequence.
    packet switching
    Sending data as packets that can take different routes, instead of holding one line open for the whole conversation.
    protocol
    An agreed set of rules for how computers talk, like grammar for machines.
    TCP/IP
    The protocols the internet uses today. IP handles addresses and routes. TCP makes sure everything arrives in order.
    RFC
    Request for Comments. A public technical note that asks for feedback. Internet standards are still written this way.
    email
    Messages sent between people’s accounts on a network. It first appeared on ARPANET in 1971.
